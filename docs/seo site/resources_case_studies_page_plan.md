# Tellann Case Studies Page — Complete SEO Marketing Specification

TELLANN · RESOURCES · CASE STUDIES

# Real software challenges. Clearer evidence. Better decisions.

Route: `/case-studies` · Page type: Customer proof and applied use-case hub · Primary objectives: Build trust, demonstrate practical value, and convert qualified prospects.

## 1. Purpose of the Case Studies page

The `/case-studies` page should help prospective Tellann customers answer one question:

“How would Tellann help a team like mine solve a problem I actually have?”

Where `/blog` shares ideas and `/research` investigates methods and evidence, `/case-studies` should demonstrate practical applications of Tellann through genuine customer stories, documented pilot results, and carefully labeled illustrative workflows.

The page should bridge three things:

1. The problem: What was the engineering, QA, or product challenge?
2. The application: How was Tellann used to investigate or address it?
3. The outcome: What was actually discovered, improved, or clarified?

The most important principle is evidence. A case study should not claim improved bug-detection rates, saved engineering hours, faster releases, or customer satisfaction unless those outcomes have been measured and the claims are authorized for publication.

If Tellann does not yet have permission to publish genuine customer stories, the page should still be useful—but it must clearly distinguish interactive examples from actual customer results.

## 3. Target audiences and the value they should recognize

| Audience              | Main concern                                     | Case-study angle                                                          |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Developers            | Difficult-to-understand application behavior     | Following a workflow from declared expectations to observed execution     |
| QA engineers          | Missing scenarios and unclear test coverage      | Identifying which expected paths remain unverified                        |
| Engineering managers  | Incomplete release-readiness evidence            | Turning scattered technical observations into an interpretable QA report  |
| Product managers      | Features that do not fully reflect requirements  | Comparing the intended user journey against actual demonstrations         |
| Startup founders      | Limited QA resources and costly mistakes         | Prioritizing critical workflows and getting useful quality evidence early |
| Enterprise evaluators | Security, repeatability, governance and adoption | Understanding deployment boundaries, controls and evaluation criteria     |

### Recommended audience-specific messaging

Developer: “See how a team investigated what its application actually did.”

QA engineer: “See how declared workflows and observed behavior expose gaps worth testing.”

Engineering manager: “See how behavioral evidence helps teams discuss release risk more precisely.”

Product manager: “See how intended user journeys can be compared with demonstrated execution.”

Founder: “See how small teams can focus their testing effort on the workflows that matter most.”

These are marketing messages describing the potential value of the workflow—not verified claims of customer outcomes.

## 8. Section 03 — Story credibility and content classification

This section is essential for an early-stage product.

### Heading

> Understand the story behind the evidence.

Create a simple classification system used throughout the page.

Verified customer story

A real customer-approved account of using Tellann, with documented evidence, attributable outcomes, and publication consent.

Documented pilot or internal study

A clearly identified evaluation with a defined environment, repeatable workflow, and disclosed limitations.

Illustrative scenario

A fictional or synthetic application example created to explain how Tellann's capabilities could be used. Not a customer success claim.

Use neutral labels—not colored badges that imply all three have equal evidentiary standing.

### Placement

Immediately after the hero or as a compact introduction above the first case-study grid.

This section should be no more than 280–340px high on desktop. On mobile, stack the three explanations.

## 14. Section 09 — The Tellann Process in Practice

### Heading

> From application intent to a clearer picture of quality.

This section should show how a customer or evaluator would move through Tellann's MVP workflow.

1. Declare intent

   Specify the behavior or workflow the application is expected to support.
2. Bind the local workspace

   Connect the relevant development workspace through Tellann's desktop workflow.
3. Review instrumentation

   Explicitly review the proposed instrumentation rather than allowing undisclosed automatic code changes.
4. Demonstrate behavior

   Execute the application journey in the managed browser environment.
5. Reconcile evidence

   Compare observed states and transitions with what was declared.
6. Review the QA report

   Understand demonstrated behavior, gaps, and areas requiring additional testing.

### Visual design

Create a horizontal six-step flow diagram with corresponding miniature product UI screenshots.

- Main diagram source: 1920×720 SVG.
- Rendered desktop: 1120×420px.
- Small screenshots: 640×400 source.
- Mobile: vertical timeline.
- Transition: line draw over approximately 2 seconds, once.
- Reduced motion: static complete diagram.

An appropriate footer link is `How Tellann Works →`, pointing to the established overview route.

## 15. Section 10 — Results and Evidence

This is where genuine case studies should become distinctly more valuable than illustrative examples.

> What the evidence showed.

For verified studies, present a compact summary of the outcome and its basis.

## 19. Section 14 — How to Read a Tellann Case Study

### Heading

> Know what each story actually demonstrates.

This short section establishes a consistent evidence standard.

Every case study should answer:

1. What application and workflow were examined?
2. What behavior was expected?
3. Which features of Tellann were actually used?
4. What execution evidence was available?
5. What did reconciliation identify?
6. What follow-up investigation occurred?
7. What conclusion is supported?
8. What remains unknown?

A case study describing incomplete observations should not end with “Tellann detected two bugs” unless those bugs were separately confirmed.

Use a minimal vertical checklist. No image required.

## 24. Detailed case-study article sections

A. Hero and executive summary

Use a strong, specific headline explaining the workflow challenge. Include a clear label identifying whether this is a customer story, pilot, or illustrative example.

The summary should explain what happened without requiring the visitor to read the full article.

B. The challenge

Describe the application architecture only to the level relevant for understanding the case. Explain the workflow, known problem, and what the team needed to establish.

C. The intended behavior

Show the declared application journey. Use a graph, step list, or annotated flow. Clearly distinguish required behavior from optional branches.

D. The Tellann workflow

Explain the actual process: workspace binding, reviewed instrumentation, managed browser demonstration, captured behavior, reconciliation, and report review, as applicable to the recorded study.

E. What was observed

Present real screenshots, a timeline, graph, or supporting evidence. Do not present an editorial illustration as an authentic capture.

F. Findings and interpretation

Separate what the evidence established from what the team inferred and what remained to be tested.

G. Outcome

Report the genuine effect of the investigation and explain how it was evaluated.

H. Limitations

Disclose missing evidence, unusual test conditions, nonrepresentative workloads, or other factors affecting conclusions.

I. Related product capabilities

Link only to features actually used or directly relevant.

### Publishing safeguards

For customer stories, require approval of the organization's identity and quotations before publication. For quantitative claims, require a data source and measurement method. For any media marked as authentic evidence, retain internal provenance and appropriate redaction records.

Prefer a CMS-driven collection rather than hardcoded case-study components.

## 29. Performance, accessibility and motion

All content must remain functional without decorative motion.

Recommended constraints:

| Interaction          | Behavior                                | Duration      |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------------- | ------------- |
| Hero illustration    | Draw paths once                         | 3–4 sec total |
| Story card hover     | Minimal image scale and border response | 200ms         |
| Filter change        | Short opacity transition                | 150–200ms     |
| Interactive workflow | User-controlled stage transitions       | 250–350ms     |
| Evidence comparison  | Controlled reveal, never auto-advancing | 250ms         |
| Screenshot lightbox  | Simple opacity/scale transition         | 180ms         |

Technical requirements:

- Responsive AVIF/WebP images.
- Lazy-loading below-the-fold content.
- Real responsive image dimensions.
- Poster-first video loading.
- Captions and transcripts.
- Accessible image enlargement.
- Keyboard-operable filters and step controls.
- Respect reduced-motion settings.
- Server-rendered story content for SEO.

## 30. Development rollout

Phase 1 — Launch with honest examples

Recommended first

Build the landing page, featured illustrative story, role-based discovery, four high-quality synthetic workflow examples, individual detail templates, an interactive walkthrough, and related product links.

Phase 2 — Document real pilots

Introduce evidence-backed internal or external pilot stories, real screen captures, outcome methodologies, and technical walkthrough videos. Replace illustrative stories in the featured position when stronger evidence exists.

Phase 3 — Customer proof library

Add approved customer stories, testimonials, measured outcomes, verified logos, industry filtering, interviews, and richer attribution. Expand only as customer adoption produces credible proof.

## 33. Final recommendation

For Tellann, the Case Studies page should be your most practical proof-oriented marketing resource, but its credibility depends on clearly separating demonstrated reality from illustrative possibility.

At launch, invest in a few outstanding educational scenarios that show exactly how declared intent, desktop demonstrations, observed behavior, reconciliation, and QA reports fit together. Use those examples to educate developers, QA engineers, managers, product teams, and founders.

As Tellann earns genuine usage evidence, add pilot studies and customer-approved stories, then increasingly organize the page around actual outcomes.

The ideal visitor journey is:

Recognize a familiar problem → See a relevant workflow → Understand the evidence → Trust the conclusion → Explore Tellann.

That is a stronger long-term conversion strategy than filling the page with unverified success metrics or generic testimonials.
